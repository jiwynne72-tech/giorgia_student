Reported speech

1. Saying what someone said 💬

We use reported speech when we tell someone what another person said, without repeating their exact words.

Direct speech: “I am tired,” Anna said.
Reported speech: Anna said (that) she was tired.

When the reporting verb is in the past (for example, said or told), the tense often moves one step back:

  • present simple → past simple: “I work” → He said he worked.
  • present continuous → past continuous: “I am working” → He said he was working.
  • will → would: “I will call” → He said he would call.
  • can → could: “I can swim” → He said he could swim.

Pronouns and time expressions may also change: my → her, today → that day, tomorrow → the following day.

SAID vs. TOLD

SAID usually has no person immediately after it: She said (that) she was busy.
TOLD needs the person who receives the information: She told me (that) she was busy.

One tiny verb, a lot of meanings

2. GET 🎁

Get is one of the most flexible verbs in English. Its meaning changes according to the words around it. That is why it can be difficult for non-native speakers: one familiar-looking verb can express ideas that require many different verbs in other languages.

Common meanings of GET

  • Receive: I got a message from Luca.
  • Obtain / buy: I need to get some milk.
  • Become: It's getting cold. / She gets nervous before exams.
  • Arrive: What time did you get home?
  • Understand: I don't get this joke.
  • Fetch: Could you get my glasses?
  • Have an experience / suffer: He got a parking ticket.
Useful pattern: get + adjective often means “become”: get tired, get better, get angry, get ready.
Get to + place means arrive: How do I get to the station?

① Quantifiers & nouns

3. Countable or uncountable? 🍓

Countable nouns have singular and plural forms. You can count them: one apple, two apples; a chair, three chairs.

Uncountable nouns are not normally counted individually. They usually have no plural form: water, information, advice, furniture, luggage, rice.

Watch out! Some nouns that are countable in other languages are uncountable in English: advice, information, news, furniture. Say a piece of advice, not an advice.

MANY vs. MUCH

  • Many + plural countable nouns: How many books have you got?
  • Much + uncountable nouns: How much time do we have?

In affirmative everyday sentences, a lot of is often more natural: I've got a lot of friends / a lot of work.

② Grammar & pronunciation fundamentals

4. Modals, contractions & the little S 🔤

Modals and contractions

Modal verbs include can, could, should, must, may, might and will. They are followed by the base form of the verb, without to (except in expressions such as ought to).

She can swim. ✓   She can to swim. ✗

Common contractions in speech and informal writing:

  • do not → don't; does not → doesn't
  • cannot → can't; will not → won't
  • should have → should've; would have → would've
In should've, could've and would've, the written contraction ends in 've, but the sound is usually /əv/ or /v/. In fast speech, the words can sound very compressed.

The third-person singular -S

In the present simple, add -s or -es to the verb with he, she or it: she works, he watches, it rains.

Pronunciation has three common endings:

  • /s/ after a voiceless sound: works, stops
  • /z/ after a voiced sound or vowel: plays, runs
  • /ɪz/ after sibilant sounds: watches, washes, chooses

Important: after does or doesn't, the main verb has no -s: Does she work? / She doesn't work.

Past tense + infinitive

Some verbs in the past are followed by a to-infinitive (to + base verb). The first verb is in the past; the infinitive describes the action, decision, plan or intention.

  • wanted to: I wanted to call you.
  • decided to: She decided to study medicine.
  • promised to: He promised to help.
  • managed to: We managed to catch the train.
  • forgot to: I forgot to lock the door.
Not every past-tense verb takes to + infinitive. For example, enjoy takes an -ing form: I enjoyed swimming, not I enjoyed to swim.

Adjectives & vocabulary

5. Interested vs. interesting ✨

Both words come from the verb interest, but they describe different things.

Interested describes how a person (or animal) feels: I am interested in astronomy.
Interesting describes the thing that creates the feeling: Astronomy is interesting.

A useful way to remember:

  • -ed = the person experiencing the feeling.
  • -ing = the thing causing the feeling.

Compare: The lesson is boring, so the students are bored. / The film is exciting, so I am excited.

Tense contrast & error correction

6. Since, for and the present perfect ⏳

Compare these two sentences:

Incorrect: I started skiing since when I was four.
Correct: I've been skiing since I was four.

I started skiing is the past simple. It describes the moment an activity began. We can say: I started skiing when I was four. We do not normally use since with this past-simple structure.

I've been skiing is the present perfect continuous. It describes an activity that began in the past and has continued up to now (or is still relevant now). Use since for the starting point and for for the duration.

  • Since + starting point: since I was four, since Monday, since 2020.
  • For + period of time: for ten years, for three days, for a long time.

More examples: I've lived here since 2021. / I've been learning English for six years.

You can also say I've skied since I was four. The present perfect simple is possible because ski can describe repeated experience. The continuous form emphasises the ongoing activity.
