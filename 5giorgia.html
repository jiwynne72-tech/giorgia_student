Lesson 5: Grammar, Vocabulary & Art 🎨

Notes from our session plus interactive practice on adjectives, movement verbs, quantifiers, and Renaissance art.

📝 Quick Notes from Our Previous Lesson

Survive (verb) To continue to live or exist, especially after a difficult or dangerous situation (e.g., "Not many characters survive every season of Grey's Anatomy!").
Fast vs. Quickly Fast is both an adjective and an adverb (a fast car / drive fast). Never say "fastly". Quickly is an adverb (finish quickly).
Lying on the couch Use lie / lying (not laying) when resting horizontally without an object ("She was lying on the couch watching TV.").
The Louvre & Portrait Artist Remember: in English it is the Louvre (masculine/neutral the, not la). A portrait artist paints portraits of people.

1. Interesting vs. Interested / Fascinating vs. Fascinated

The Golden Rule for Participle Adjectives:
  • -ING adjectives (interesting, fascinating, boring, tiring, exciting) describe the cause — the person, thing, or situation that produces the feeling.
    Example: "Masaccio's frescoes are fascinating." (The frescoes cause the feeling).
  • -ED adjectives (interested, fascinated, bored, tired, excited) describe the feeling or emotion a person experiences.
    Example: "Giorgia is fascinated by Renaissance art." (Giorgia feels the emotion).
1. We watched an episode of Grey's Anatomy last night, and the medical case was really _______.
2. I have always been _______ in how Renaissance artists discovered linear perspective.
3. The tour guide at the Louvre told us a _______ story about a portrait artist.
4. The students stood in front of the Brancacci Chapel, completely _______ by the colors.
5. Walking around a huge museum for four hours can be very _______.

2. Bring vs. Take (Direction of Movement)

Think about the listener's or speaker's location:
  • BRING = movement towards the speaker or listener (here / where we are or will be).
    Example: "Can you bring your English notebook to our next lesson?" (towards the teacher/class).
  • TAKE = movement away from the speaker or listener (there / to another place).
    Example: "Don't forget to take an umbrella when you leave the house." (away from here).
1. When you come to my house later, please _______ your sketchbook so I can see your drawings.
2. We are going to Florence tomorrow, and I'm going to _______ my camera to photograph the churches.
3. Could you _______ me a glass of water while I'm lying here on the couch?
4. After class, remember to _______ your jacket with you—it's cold outside!
5. My friend went to Paris and promised to _______ me back a postcard from the Louvre.

3. Quantifiers: Too, So Much, A Lot, Enough & Many

Key Rules to Remember:
  • TOO / TOO MUCH / TOO MANY = more than necessary or wanted (usually a negative problem).
    Use too + adjective (too fast), too much + uncountable noun (too much homework), too many + countable plural noun (too many tourists).
  • SO / SO MUCH / SO MANY = a very high degree (used for strong emphasis, often positive or dramatic).
    Use so + adjective (so good), so much + uncountable noun/verb (I love art so much), so many + countable plural noun (so many paintings).
  • A LOT (OF) / LOTS OF = a large quantity (works in positive sentences with both countable & uncountable nouns). Use a lot at the end of a sentence without a noun ("She studies a lot").
  • ENOUGH = the right amount. Goes before nouns (enough time) and after adjectives (fast enough).
1. There were _______ tourists in front of the Mona Lisa that we couldn't even see it!
2. Don't speak _______ quickly, please—I'm trying to write everything down in my notes.
3. Thank you _______ for helping me understand the difference between bring and take!
4. We didn't have _______ time to visit the entire museum in a single afternoon.
5. If you drink _______ coffee late at night, you won't be able to sleep.
6. Masaccio accomplished _______ in his short life, dying at just twenty-six years old.

4. Reading: Masaccio and the Birth of Renaissance Perspective

Tommaso di Ser Giovanni di Mòne (1401–1428), famously known as Masaccio, was one of the most revolutionary painters of the early Italian Renaissance. Although his life was tragically short, he transformed Western art by moving away from the flat, decorative Gothic style and introducing realistic human emotion, natural lighting, and mathematical linear perspective.

His greatest masterpiece can be found in the Brancacci Chapel in Florence, where he painted a series of frescoes depicting scenes from the life of Saint Peter—most notably The Tribute Money, Saint Peter Healing the Sick with His Shadow, and The Distribution of Alms and Death of Ananias. In these three scenes, Masaccio gave his figures real physical weight and placed them in believable architectural spaces, inspiring generations of artists including Michelangelo and Raphael.
