1. The Clockmaker's Secret

Read the fable. Look out for the highlighted grammar patterns.

In a small village, there lived a clockmaker called Elias. He used to open his workshop at sunrise and would spend the first hour listening to the clocks. He believed that every clock had its own voice. These days, however, he is used to working late because customers often arrive after work.

One morning, a young traveller brought him a beautiful old clock. “It has stopped,” she said. “I've tried winding it, but nothing happens. Could you help?”

Elias examined the clock carefully. “I remember seeing one like this when I was an apprentice,” he said. “My teacher told me to be patient and never force a mechanism.” He smiled. “I stopped repairing clocks in a hurry a long time ago. Rushing usually creates more problems.”

He began to work, but the tiny gears were difficult to reach. He tried using a thinner tool. It worked for a moment, then the mechanism jammed again. “Perhaps I need to try taking the back panel off,” he thought.

The traveller grew impatient. “Why don't you just make it work?” she asked.

“Because,” Elias replied, “a good craftsperson must learn to listen before trying to fix something. I used to think that speed was the most important thing. Then I damaged a very valuable clock. That mistake taught me to slow down.”

At last, Elias found a tiny piece of dust caught between two gears. He removed it, and the clock began to tick. The traveller was delighted. “I regret bringing it here so late in the day,” she said, “but I'm glad I did.”

“Never regret asking for help,” Elias answered. “And remember to take care of the things you value.”

As the traveller left, she noticed that the clock was not showing the correct time. “Oh no! It says five minutes past six, but it's nearly noon!”

Elias laughed. “A clock can tell you the time, but it cannot tell you how to use it. That's something you have to learn for yourself.”

Fable's message: Patience and careful attention are often more useful than speed. A mistake can teach us something valuable, provided we are willing to learn from it.

2. Grammar notes

A. Present and past habits

used to + infinitive — a past habit or state that is no longer true.
“Elias used to open his workshop at sunrise.”
would + infinitive — repeated past actions, often in a story. It is not normally used for past states.
“He would spend the first hour listening to the clocks.”
be used to + noun / -ing — to be accustomed to something. It can refer to the present or the past.
“He is used to working late.”
get used to + noun / -ing — to become accustomed to something.
“He is getting used to working later.”

B. Verb patterns: infinitive or -ing?

Some verbs are followed by the infinitive with to; others are followed by the -ing form. Learn the pattern with the verb.

Verb + -ing: enjoy, avoid, finish, suggest, keep, mind.
“He stopped repairing clocks in a hurry.”
Verb + to-infinitive: want, decide, hope, promise, learn, manage.
“His teacher told him to be patient.”
Verb + object + to-infinitive: tell, ask, advise, encourage.
“His teacher told him to be patient.”

C. When the pattern changes the meaning

stop doing = finish an activity.
“He stopped repairing clocks in a hurry.”
stop to do = pause one activity in order to do another.
“He stopped to answer the phone.”
remember doing = have a memory of something that happened.
“I remember seeing that clock.”
remember to do = not forget a task.
“Remember to lock the workshop.”
try doing = experiment with a possible solution.
“Try using a thinner tool.”
try to do = make an effort to do something difficult.
“He tried to open the case, but it was stuck.”
regret doing = feel sorry about something you did.
“I regret bringing it so late.”
regret to do = formally announce something unpleasant.
“We regret to inform you that the workshop is closed.”
Tip: The word after the verb matters. Sometimes it changes the grammar; sometimes it changes the meaning too.

4. Grammar practice

Complete each sentence with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

1. When I was younger, I ______ (visit) my grandparents every Sunday.

2. I'm not ______ (work) at weekends, so Saturday feels strange.

3. Please remember ______ (lock) the door before you leave.

4. I remember ______ (meet) her at the station years ago.

5. The computer wasn't working, so I tried ______ (restart) it.

6. He tried ______ (lift) the heavy box, but it was too heavy.

7. She stopped ______ (talk) when the teacher entered.

8. We stopped ______ (buy) some water on our way home.

Show the grammar practice answers
  1. used to visit / would visit
  2. used to working
  3. to lock
  4. meeting
  5. restarting
  6. to lift
  7. talking
  8. to buy

For question 1, both answers are possible. For question 5, “to restart” is also possible if the meaning is making an effort rather than experimenting with a solution.
